## salary · single
"Salary" comes from Latin *salarium*. What is the story usually told about it, and how true is it?

- [x] It is linked to salt (*sal*), but the idea that Roman soldiers were paid in salt is a legend: *salarium* was a money allowance
- [ ] Roman soldiers really were paid in bags of salt, which is why "worth his salt" exists
- [ ] It comes from *salire*, to jump, because wages "jump" every year
- [ ] It comes from the Salian Franks, who invented regular pay

> No Roman text describes soldiers paid in salt. The link to salt is real but unclear: perhaps an allowance to buy it, perhaps a payment to guard the salt roads. "Worth his salt" only appears in the 19th century.

## muscle · single
What animal is hiding in the word "muscle"?

- [x] A mouse: Latin *musculus*, "little mouse", because a muscle moving under the skin looks like one
- [ ] A mussel, because of the shape of the biceps
- [ ] A mule, for its strength
- [ ] A muskrat

> The Romans had the same image, and so do other languages: in Greek, *mys* means both mouse and muscle. And the mussel? It comes from the same *musculus*: a little mouse of the sea.

## nice · single
In the 14th century, what did it mean to call someone "nice"?

- [x] That they were foolish or ignorant, from Latin *nescius*, "not knowing"
- [ ] That they were kind and pleasant
- [ ] That they came from the city of Nice
- [ ] That they were rich

> The word then drifted: foolish, then fussy, then precise (a "nice distinction" is still a subtle one), and only in the 18th century, pleasant.

## silly · single
What did "silly" (Old English *sælig*) originally mean?

- [x] Blessed, happy
- [ ] Silent
- [ ] Made of silk
- [ ] Lazy

> Blessed, then innocent, then pitiable, then foolish: a slow slide over a thousand years. The German *selig*, "blessed", kept the original meaning.

## clue · single
Why do detectives follow "clues"?

- [x] A *clew* was a ball of thread, like the one Ariadne gave Theseus to find his way out of the Minotaur's labyrinth
- [ ] It comes from *clueless*, a police slang word
- [ ] It comes from the French *clou*, a nail, used to pin evidence on a board
- [ ] It is an abbreviation of "clear evidence"

> Following the thread led Theseus out of the maze; following a clue leads the detective to the solution. The spelling *clue* became common in the 17th century.

## quarantine · single
Why "quarantine"?

- [x] In 14th-century Venice, ships from plague-stricken ports had to wait forty days (*quaranta giorni*) before landing
- [ ] It was the name of the doctor who invented isolation
- [ ] It comes from a Latin word meaning "to hide"
- [ ] Patients were kept in groups of forty

> The rule was first applied, with thirty days, in Ragusa (Dubrovnik) in 1377, then extended to forty in Venice. Forty days also echoed Lent and the forty days of the Flood.

## deadline · single
Where does "deadline" come from?

- [x] From a line around a prison camp during the American Civil War: prisoners who crossed it could be shot
- [ ] From the printing trade: the last line of a newspaper column
- [ ] From the time of death written on a certificate
- [ ] From the finish line of a horse race

> The deadline of the Andersonville camp (1864) is the best known. Printers later used the word for a line not to be crossed on the press bed, and then the meaning "time limit" appeared in journalism, in the 1920s.

## goodbye · single
What is "goodbye" a contraction of?

- [x] "God be with ye"
- [ ] "Good buy", a merchants' farewell
- [ ] "Good by now"
- [ ] "Go to bye", a sailors' expression

> In Shakespeare's time, people wrote *God b'wy you* or *God buy you*. Under the influence of "good day" and "good night", *God* became *good*. The French *adieu* ("to God") follows the same idea.

## apron · single
Why do we say "an apron" and not "a napron", as in the Middle Ages?

- [x] People heard "a napron" as "an apron": the *n* moved from the noun to the article
- [ ] It was a printing mistake in the first English dictionary
- [ ] A royal decree changed the word in 1500
- [ ] "Napron" was considered impolite

> *Napron* came from the French *naperon*, a little tablecloth (*nappe*, a napkin). The same slip gave "an adder" (from *a nadder*) and "an umpire" (from *a noumpere*). It also worked the other way: *an ekename*, "an extra name", became "a nickname".

## daisy · single
What does the name of the daisy describe?

- [x] Its habit of opening at dawn and closing at dusk: *day's eye*
- [ ] Its white petals, like a daisy chain of pearls
- [ ] A girl called Daisy who first grew it
- [ ] Its short life, a single day

> Old English *dæges ēage*, "day's eye". The flower's yellow heart is the sun, and its petals the eyelids that open in the morning.

## window · single
What is the "window" literally?

- [x] The "wind eye", from Old Norse *vindauga*: an opening in the roof that let the smoke out and the air in
- [ ] A "wide door"
- [ ] A "winding hole", for a spiral staircase
- [ ] A "wooden view"

> A Viking word that replaced the Old English *ēagþyrel*, "eye-hole". Early windows had no glass.

## posh · single
"Posh" is often explained as "Port Out, Starboard Home", the shady cabins of wealthy passengers sailing to India. What do etymologists think?

- [x] It is a myth: no ticket bearing these letters has ever been found, and the true origin is uncertain
- [ ] It is well attested by P&O shipping tickets
- [ ] It comes from the Persian word for "royal"
- [ ] It is an abbreviation of "polished shoes"

> The acronym explanation appeared long after the word, a classic "backronym". "Posh" may come from an old slang word for money, or a dandy. Distrust explanations by acronym: very few English words were born that way.
