## sandwich · single
Who gave his name to the sandwich?

- [x] John Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich, in the 18th century, who reportedly asked for meat between two slices of bread so that he could eat without leaving his desk, or his card table
- [ ] A baker from the town of Sandwich, in Kent
- [ ] A sailor shipwrecked on the Sandwich Islands
- [ ] An Italian cook named Sandovici

> The gambling version comes from a French traveller's account of London, published in 1765; his biographers think he was more likely working. Either way, the name stuck. Captain Cook named the Sandwich Islands (Hawaii) after the same earl.

## boycott · single
Who was Boycott?

- [x] Charles Boycott, a land agent in Ireland who, in 1880, was shunned by the whole community after he evicted tenants
- [ ] A shopkeeper who refused to sell to soldiers
- [ ] An American president who banned British goods
- [ ] A cricket player who refused to play

> The Irish Land League, led by Charles Parnell, encouraged tenants to isolate unfair landlords rather than use violence. Within a year, *boycott* had entered French, German, Dutch and Russian.

## cardigan · single
Where does the "cardigan" come from?

- [x] James Brudenell, 7th Earl of Cardigan, a British officer of the Crimean War, who wore a knitted wool waistcoat
- [ ] A Welsh shepherd who invented it
- [ ] The town of Cardigan, famous for its wool mills
- [ ] A French word for a carder's jacket

> The same earl led the disastrous Charge of the Light Brigade at Balaklava, in 1854. The battle also gave its name to the "balaclava", a knitted hood said to have kept the soldiers warm.

## meat · multiple
After 1066, the Norman lords ate the meat that English peasants raised. Which meat words come from Norman French?

- [x] beef (*bœuf*)
- [x] pork (*porc*)
- [x] mutton (*mouton*)
- [x] veal (*veau*)
- [ ] lamb

> The animals in the fields kept their English names (*cow, pig, sheep, calf*), the meat on the lord's table took French ones. The story, popularised by Walter Scott in *Ivanhoe*, is simplified, but the pattern is real. *Lamb* (like *chicken*) is English for both the animal and the meat.

## days · single
Which god is hiding in "Wednesday"?

- [x] Woden (Odin), the chief god of the Germanic peoples
- [ ] Mercury, the Roman messenger god
- [ ] Wotan's wife, Frigg
- [ ] Saturn

> The English days are mostly Germanic gods: **Tuesday** (Tiw), **Wednesday** (Woden), **Thursday** (Thor), **Friday** (Frigg). The Romans gave the day to Mercury, hence French *mercredi*: Woden was seen as the Germanic equivalent of Mercury. **Saturday** kept the Roman Saturn.

## jeans-denim · multiple
Which statements about jeans are true?

- [x] "Jeans" comes from *Gênes*, the French name of Genoa, whose sailors wore trousers of strong cotton cloth
- [x] "Denim" comes from *serge de Nîmes*, a fabric from the French city of Nîmes
- [ ] Levi Strauss invented both the word and the cloth
- [ ] "Jeans" comes from Jean, a French tailor

> Levi Strauss and the tailor Jacob Davis patented riveted work trousers in 1873, but the words are much older. Two French place names hide in your wardrobe.

## robot · single
Where does the word "robot" come from?

- [x] From a Czech play by Karel Čapek, *R.U.R.* (1920), from *robota*, forced labour
- [ ] From an American science-fiction magazine
- [ ] From the name of the first industrial machine
- [ ] From Greek *rhombos*, a spinning wheel

> Čapek's robots were artificial workers who rebelled against humans. He credited his brother Josef with the word. Isaac Asimov later coined "robotics".

## bedlam · single
Why does "bedlam" mean chaos and uproar?

- [x] It is a contraction of Bethlem, the Hospital of St Mary of Bethlehem in London, which housed the mentally ill
- [ ] It comes from a sheep pen ("lamb's bed") at lambing time
- [ ] It was the name of a noisy London market
- [ ] It comes from a Norse word for a storm

> Founded in 1247, the hospital became infamous for its conditions; in the 18th century, visitors paid to look at the patients. It still exists, as the Bethlem Royal Hospital.

## tawdry · single
"Tawdry" means cheap and showy. Which saint is hiding in it?

- [x] Saint Audrey (Æthelthryth): cheap lace sold at her fair in Ely was called "St Audrey's lace"
- [ ] Saint Tawd, patron saint of tailors
- [ ] Saint Andrew, by a Scottish mispronunciation
- [ ] Saint Theodore

> *Seynt Audrey lace* became *tawdry lace*, then simply *tawdry*. Another case of a word cut in the wrong place, like "an apron".

## ok · single
What is the most widely accepted origin of "OK"?

- [x] A joke abbreviation of "oll korrect", a misspelling of "all correct", in Boston newspapers in 1839, popularised by the 1840 presidential campaign of "Old Kinderhook" Martin Van Buren
- [ ] The Choctaw word *okeh*, "it is so"
- [ ] "Aux quais", French sailors' word in American ports
- [ ] The initials of a railway clerk, Obadiah Kelly

> The linguist Allen Walker Read established this origin in the 1960s, from newspaper archives. Abbreviations of misspellings were a fashion of the time: *OW* for "oll wright", *KY* for "know yuse"… Only *OK* survived.

## mortgage · single
What does "mortgage" mean, word for word, in Old French?

- [x] A "dead pledge": the pledge dies when the debt is paid, or is lost if it is not
- [ ] A "death wage", paid to widows
- [ ] A "mortar gauge", used by builders
- [ ] A "bitter debt"

> *Mort* (dead) + *gage* (pledge). The *t* is still written, and still silent, as in French. English law is full of Norman French: *bailiff, attorney, plaintiff, jury, verdict*.

## curfew · single
What did the medieval "curfew" order people to do?

- [x] To cover their fires at night: *couvre-feu*
- [ ] To close the city gates
- [ ] To ring the church bells
- [ ] To go to church

> A bell rang in the evening to remind people to cover the hearth, to prevent fires in wooden towns. The obligation to stay indoors came later. French still says *couvre-feu*.
